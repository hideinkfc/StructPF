Generating Productive Failure

A bounded design-data pipeline for tasks that invite meaningful approaches and comparison.

Master thesis · October 10, 2026 · Interactive dashboard · Current research record
Research question

Not a hard problem — a productive one

A Productive Failure task should let students start with what they know, encounter useful limitations, and leave material for comparison. Partial and successful approaches can also contribute.

What will students try?
What does each approach reveal or leave unresolved?
How can we check the task before calling it PF?
Historical attempt 1 — direct generation

Asking the model for a PF exercise directly

  • The numbers accidentally make the shortcut valid — the trap disappears.
  • A "wrong" method turns out mathematically correct.
  • The narrative announces the error instead of eliciting it.
Historical example of a narrated error

Maya and Leo's recipe is 2 cups lemon juice to 5 cups water. They have 3 cups of juice. Leo says: "add 1 more cup of each and it'll taste the same." Maya disagrees: "2 to 5 isn't 3 to 6." Design a plan so the lemonade tastes exactly like Grandma's.

Design concern: the story names the error (add-to-both) and has a character refute it. The goal is settled by keeping the 2:5 ratio (3 cups juice → 7.5 cups water). Different representations are possible, but the task may remain routine; this inspection is not observed student evidence.

Diagnosis

The jobs were in the wrong order

old   write a story  →  hope the design works  →  list mistakes
new   select a brief  →  data + conditions  →  check / repair  →  write

Separate design from prose. Check a numerical contrast when the design requires one; do not require every familiar method to fail.

Historical attempt 2 — MCQ error generation

Multiple-choice questions (MCQs) as an error instrument

  • Best reported match to documented Eedi errors was about 22%; higher means closer reference alignment, not better PF design.
  • Wrong answers do not capture all useful prior tools, diagrams, qualitative insights, or correct-but-insufficient approaches.
  • Structured paths make model-generated error hypotheses inspectable. They do not demonstrate observed student thinking or reliable behavioral prediction.

MCQ is a design instrument, not the primary D1 source. Behavioral probes remain proposed evaluation work; a full pathway-to-PF run has not established student outcomes.

Current default implementation

Select a brief, then run the bounded data loop

  1. D1Anticipate — curriculum / textbook grounding supports prior-resource candidates; first moves are model hypotheses.
  2. D2aSelect a design brief — goal, relationships, allowed actions, possible approaches, learning opportunity, and requirements.
  3. D2bPropose given data + conditions → restricted exact calculations and quoted model textual judgments.
  4. LoopRepair known essential numerical failures — values only, with rechecking; at most 3 data calls, including invalid-proposal retries.
  5. D3Fixed-data writer — preserve givens and rules; keep teacher-only checks and anticipated methods out of the student task.

No universal quantity translation or separate mathematical-plan gate. D4 names verification work, not a mandatory later trap test in the default route.

Current D1 sourcing

Two ways to source the prior methods

The same target and learner context can be paired with different sources of prior-resource candidates.

Grounded  ·  retrieve curriculum / textbook excerpts → map to taught-skill candidates → retain provenance and coverage limits.

Ungrounded  ·  the model proposes what students might know from the description. Provenance is model-only, not curricular evidence.

The corpus uses official curricula and open textbook excerpts. Current D1 excludes expert-resource lists from generation; that policy does not demonstrate an independent recovery advantage. Model expansion adds activation notes and first moves, not complete solution methods or evidence of what students actually do.

Illustrative example — not the final live speed task

A two-plan teaching example

Quantities  two plans; each a list of legs; each leg a distance and a time.

Question  "Which plan covers more distance per unit of total elapsed time — or are they equally effective?"

This simplified example illustrates a possible numerical contrast. It is not a selected current design or evidence of open-endedness. A current brief also specifies rules, approaches, learning opportunity, and requirements.

Illustrative prompt — D3 · the story

The story comes last

Choose the Better Route. A delivery robot can use Plan A or Plan B, each with two legs (distance in kilometres and elapsed time in hours shown). Decide which plan covers more distance per unit of total elapsed time — or whether they are equally effective. Show and explain your reasoning.

The prompt does not prescribe a formula. That alone does not make it PF: it may still invite one routine computation. The production writer preserves fixed givens and must not invent missing assumptions or turn model predictions into student facts.

Current continuation policy

Known failure and missing evidence are different

known essential numerical failure  →  values-only repair → recheck
known essential textual error      →  design review
missing support / unknown result   →  visible warning → continue
no known essential failure        →  fixed-data writer

Warnings continue even for essential claims. Textual failures take precedence when both kinds of failure occur; uncertainty does not hide a numerical failure.

Meanings, units, data sizes, conditions, and formulas freeze after a valid proposal. The 3-data-call budget includes invalid replies. “Accepted” permits writing; it does not mean every claim was verified. Textual judgments use quoted source wording and remain model judgments.

What is left for students to discover?
