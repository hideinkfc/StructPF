\chapter*{Abstract}
Productive Failure (PF) is a learning design in which students attempt a challenging problem before they are taught the target concept. A good PF task lets students start from what they already know and try several approaches, but chooses its numbers so that these familiar methods fall short and students see why the target concept is needed. Such tasks are costly to design, and few published examples exist. While a large language model (LLM) could be prompted to write PF tasks in one pass, such prompting mostly produces polished tasks that look like PF on the surface but do not work like it. In our replication of this approach, the generated tasks scored high on the rubric from prior work, yet most of them could be solved with methods students already knew.

To close this gap, we have developed \textsc{StructPF}, a pipeline that follows the design principles of PF. It retrieves the students' prior knowledge from curriculum data, selects task numbers for which a familiar method falls short and verifies them by calculation, and only then writes the task for students. Since prior rubrics only evaluate PF tasks on a surface level, we also developed \textsc{PF Eval}, a benchmark that tests the in-depth structure of a task instead.

Our evaluation showed that familiar methods fall short in four of five \textsc{StructPF} tasks compared with one in ten one-shot tasks, and that curriculum data keeps the generator from assuming that students already know the target concept. \textsc{StructPF} still trails published expert tasks, and simulated students did not reproduce the approaches of real students. These results suggest that PF generation is limited by knowledge about learners rather than by model size. LLMs are trained to solve tasks, not to anticipate how students think before they are taught, so this knowledge has to come from curricula and experts, and \textsc{StructPF} shows one way to bring it in.
