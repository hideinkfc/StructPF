\chapter{\textsc{PF Eval}: Measures, Judges, and Validation}
\label{chap:eval-framework}

\section{From Expert Review to Automated Checks}
\label{sec:eval-overview}

As shown in Chapter~\ref{chap:oneshot}, a surface rubric judge cannot tell whether a generated problem actually works as a PF task. An expert teacher can spot missing impasses, method giveaways, and mathematical errors by hand, but manual review does not scale across hundreds of generated tasks. We therefore introduce \textsc{PF Eval}, an automated evaluation framework that tests whether a generated task satisfies the underlying requirements of Productive Failure rather than how convincing its text reads.

Just as \textsc{StructPF} mirrors the four decisions a human designer makes to construct a PF task (Section~\ref{sec:structpf-overview}), \textsc{PF Eval} turns the PF requirements from Section~\ref{sec:intro-motivation} into the separate questions an expert asks when reviewing a finished task. Each question receives its own outcome, following decomposed checklist evaluations such as CheckEval~\cite{lee2025checkeval} and TICK~\cite{cook2024tick}. Figure~\ref{fig:eval-overview} summarizes these four review questions and the automated checks that answer them.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig5_1_eval_overview.pdf}
    \caption[Mapping Human PF Design Questions to the Five \textsc{PF Eval} Checks]{Mapping Human PF Design Questions to the Five \textsc{PF Eval} Checks}
    \label{fig:eval-overview}
\end{figure}

Three of the four questions in Figure~\ref{fig:eval-overview} can be checked directly from the task text and its numbers, whereas Requirement~2 cannot, because a judge that only reads the task never tries the familiar methods on the given values. Simulated students address this by letting an LLM attempt each item while playing a student with a given knowledge profile~\cite{lu2024,scarlatos2025smart}, but capable models in mathematics frequently leak knowledge beyond the assigned profile or abandon their errors (Section~\ref{subsec:bg-eval-simstudents}). More fundamentally, Requirement~2 asks about the task rather than about the students. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:eval-simstudent-vs-solver}a, even when a simulated student stays within its profile, a solved or failed attempt remains ambiguous between student invention, leaked knowledge, and deliberate role-play mistakes. Our prior-knowledge solver (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-simstudent-vs-solver}b) instead tests the task directly by doing its best with only the listed prior procedures and no deliberate errors, while a separate attempt judge that knows the target concept sets aside any attempt that uses an unlisted concept. Section~\ref{sec:eval-five-measures} defines the five task-design measures behind Figure~\ref{fig:eval-overview}, Section~\ref{sec:eval-three-roles} describes the prior-knowledge solver and the two judges, and Section~\ref{sec:eval-validation} validates \textsc{PF Eval} as a whole.

\end{figure}

\section{Five Task-Design Measures}
\label{sec:eval-five-measures}

To evaluate a generated task against the questions in Figure~\ref{fig:eval-overview}, we need measures that show which requirement holds and which fails. We have chosen to evaluate each question as a separate measure with its own outcome (\emph{Yes}, \emph{No}, or \emph{Unclear}) rather than summing points into a single score, because a high total can hide a failure on one requirement. Table~\ref{tab:eval-five-measures} defines the five measures, grouped into two task soundness checks and three PF requirement checks. The two task soundness measures verify that the problem addresses the target concept with consistent mathematics, since a task that teaches the wrong concept or contradicts itself cannot be productive, while the remaining three test PF Requirements~1 to~3 directly.

\begin{table}[!htb]
\centering
\caption[The Five Task-Design Measures in \textsc{PF Eval}]{The Five Task-Design Measures in \textsc{PF Eval}}
\label{tab:eval-five-measures}
\vspace{0.5em}
\footnotesize
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{8pt}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.28\linewidth} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Measure} & \textbf{Definition} \\
\midrule
Target alignment & Solving the task's central problem requires the target concept, even when the task never names it. \\
\addlinespace[5pt]
Mathematical validity & The givens are consistent with each other, and the task has at least one solution. \\
\cmidrule(l{0pt}r{0pt}){1-2}
Prior-knowledge accessibility (Req.~1) & A student can make a meaningful start on the task using the listed prior skills and basic arithmetic. \\
\addlinespace[5pt]
Limits of familiar approaches (Req.~2) & A familiar method, carried out correctly, falls short for the reason that the target concept resolves. \\
\addlinespace[5pt]
Room for invention (Req.~3) & The task leaves its central construction, such as a measure, model or fair rule, for students to build. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

We evaluate the five measures in a fixed order so that a failed task soundness check or an incomplete problem statement is not confused with a failed PF requirement. In particular, we check mathematical validity only when a task is aligned with the target concept, and we test the limits of familiar approaches only when the task is accessible with prior knowledge, because a familiar method cannot meaningfully fall short on a problem that students cannot even start. When an earlier check fails, the dependent measure is recorded as \emph{not assessed} rather than \emph{No}. Similarly, when essential context such as a referenced diagram is missing, we return \emph{Unclear} so that uncertainty in the judge is not counted as a confirmed flaw in the task.

\section{Evaluation Judges and Prior-Knowledge Solver}
\label{sec:eval-three-roles}

To score the five measures, \textsc{PF Eval} divides the evaluation across the three LLM roles shown in Figure~\ref{fig:eval-three-roles}, where the task judge evaluates the four text-based measures (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-three-roles}a) and the prior-knowledge solver and attempt judge together evaluate the \textbf{limits of familiar approaches} (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-three-roles}b). In our experiments, the task judge and attempt judge use GPT 5.6 Luna, while the prior-knowledge solver pools attempts from GPT 5.6 Luna and GPT-4.1.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.92\textwidth]{figures/fig5_3_three_roles.pdf}
    \caption[Information Separation in \textsc{PF Eval}: (a) Task Judge and (b) Solver with Attempt Judge]{Information Separation in \textsc{PF Eval}: (a)~Task Judge and (b)~Solver with Attempt Judge}
    \label{fig:eval-three-roles}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Task Judge}
\label{subsec:eval-task-judge}

The task judge takes the student task, the listed prior skills, and the target concept as input without seeing any solver attempts, and outputs an outcome of \emph{Yes}, \emph{No}, or \emph{Unclear} together with a brief explanation and supporting evidence for four of the five measures in Table~\ref{tab:eval-five-measures} (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-three-roles}a).
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=0.6cm,itemsep=0.4em,topsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Target Alignment}\\[0.2em]
    Checks whether answering the task's central question requires the target concept.
    \item \textbf{Prior-Knowledge Accessibility}\\[0.2em]
    Checks whether a student can take at least one concrete mathematical step toward the requested answer using the listed prior skills and basic arithmetic, or whether even the first step requires an untaught concept.
    \item \textbf{Room for Invention}\\[0.2em]
    Checks whether the prompt leaves the central mathematical construction open for students to build or states the target formula or step-by-step recipe in the text.
    \item \textbf{Mathematical Validity}\\[0.2em]
    Checks whether the given numbers and conditions are consistent and admit a solution by verifying the numerical claims with the \texttt{SymPy} calculator from Section~\ref{subsec:d2b-numbers}.
\end{enumerate}

For the first three text-based measures, the judge must also output verbatim quotes from the student task that support its explanation, and our evaluation code verifies that every quote appears word for word in the task text so the judge cannot claim a starting step or a hint that is not written in the prompt.

\subsection{Prior-Knowledge Solver}
\label{subsec:eval-solver}

On tasks that pass the accessibility check, the prior-knowledge solver receives the student task and the listed prior skills while the target concept is withheld, so that it cannot steer towards the untaught formula (Figure~\ref{fig:eval-three-roles}b). It generates between two and four complete attempts that each answer every part of the task. We divide these attempts into two groups because a single familiar method and a combined strategy test different aspects of the task design.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=0.6cm,itemsep=0.4em,topsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Familiar Methods}\\[0.2em]
    The first attempts apply a single listed method on its own, such as comparing only the means or only the ranges in the consistency task, and record where that method falls short.
    \item \textbf{Constructed Approaches}\\[0.2em]
    The remaining attempts combine several listed skills or apply general problem-solving heuristics, such as guessing and testing candidate values or organizing the givens in a table~\cite{polya1945}, to see whether a working solution can be constructed from prior knowledge.
\end{enumerate}

When the listed prior skills already include a procedure that solves the task directly, the solver applies that taught procedure across all parts of the problem rather than inventing an artificially weakened baseline, ensuring that a routine textbook exercise is not mistaken for a PF task. Each attempt states the mathematical principle it relies on and any assumptions it makes, and the solver may call the calculator one arithmetic step at a time, just as a student would use a pocket calculator.

\subsection{Attempt Judge}
\label{subsec:eval-attempt-judge}

Because the solver is blind to the target concept, a separate attempt judge inspects each attempt against the target concept to evaluate the \textbf{limits of familiar approaches} and assigns one of the five labels shown in Figure~\ref{fig:eval-attempt-labels}a.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/fig5_3b_attempt_labels.pdf}
    \caption[Attempt-Judge Labels and Priority Rule for the Limits of Familiar Approaches]{Attempt-Judge Labels~(a) and Priority Rule for the Limits of Familiar Approaches~(b)}
    \label{fig:eval-attempt-labels}
\end{figure}

Code then derives the outcome for the \textbf{limits of familiar approaches} using the priority rule in Figure~\ref{fig:eval-attempt-labels}b. After setting aside any \emph{Unrelated shortfall} or \emph{Unsupported} attempts, a single \emph{Target limit} is enough for \emph{Yes} even if a later constructed attempt also \emph{Meets demands}, whereas a routine task where familiar methods reach \emph{Meets demands} directly without any \emph{Target limit} receives \emph{No}.

\section{Validating \textsc{PF Eval}}
\label{sec:eval-validation}

\textsc{PF Eval} relies on the prior-knowledge solver and the assumption that its attempts cover the familiar methods a student can try with the listed skills. If the solver overlooks a familiar method that falls short, \textsc{PF Eval} would reject a working PF task, and if it misses a direct solution from prior knowledge, \textsc{PF Eval} would accept a routine exercise. Beyond the solver, the two judges must also apply the five measures accurately on the finished task. Before comparing generators in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}, we therefore validate \textsc{PF Eval} in two steps, testing first whether the prior-knowledge solver recovers the expected approaches on its own (Section~\ref{subsec:val-solver}) and second whether \textsc{PF Eval} as a whole accepts published PF tasks and rejects flawed tasks for the right reason (Section~\ref{subsec:val-known-tasks}).

\subsection{Solver Recall on Expected Approaches}
\label{subsec:val-solver}

In \textsc{PF Eval}, the prior-knowledge solver receives only the student task and the listed prior skills and generates only two to four attempts per model. We therefore need to verify whether this small sample is sufficient to surface the specific approaches a task is built around. To test this across both human-written and generated problems, we constructed a benchmark of 76 tasks with 256 expected approaches recorded in advance. The benchmark spans three task types, namely 12 published PF tasks with 28 student approaches documented in classroom studies, 12 routine and control tasks with 19 standard solutions, and 52 \textsc{StructPF} tasks with 209 approaches planned in Stage~2a (Section~\ref{subsec:d2a-brief}). We run the solver exactly as in \textsc{PF Eval}, pooling attempts from GPT 5.6 Luna and GPT-4.1 without tuning on this benchmark, and use a matching judge to check whether each expected approach is carried out.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{figures/fig5_4_solver_recall.pdf}
    \caption[Recall of Expected Student Approaches]{Recall of Expected Student Approaches}
    \label{fig:val-solver-recall}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:val-solver-recall}, the solver recovers $85.5\%$ of the expected approaches overall, with nearly all misses occurring on the pipeline-generated tasks whose planned approaches were written by the generator rather than observed in students. More importantly for task evaluation, on every PF task in the benchmark (including all published tasks), the solver tries at least one familiar method that falls short as required for a \emph{Yes} on the limits of familiar approaches, and on every routine task it finds the direct solution without inventing artificial failures. Manual inspection of a 12-task sample also confirmed all matching decisions.

\subsection{Validating \textsc{PF Eval} on Reference Tasks}
\label{subsec:val-known-tasks}
\label{subsec:val-good-pass}
\label{subsec:val-flawed-fail}

Knowing that the solver finds the expected approaches is only half of the validation. We also need to verify that the five measures and three roles work together as a complete evaluator, so that \textsc{PF Eval} is neither too strict to pass real PF tasks nor too lenient to catch specific design flaws. We therefore evaluate \textsc{PF Eval} on the 29 reference tasks with known expected outcomes in Table~\ref{tab:eval-known-tasks}, and Figure~\ref{fig:val-reference-outcomes} reports the resulting non-passing counts by group.

The outcomes in Figure~\ref{fig:val-reference-outcomes} confirm both requirements for \textsc{PF Eval} to be reliable:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=0.6cm,label=\textbf{\arabic*.},itemsep=0.6em,topsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Accepting Published PF Tasks}\\[0.2em]
    As shown in the top row of Figure~\ref{fig:val-reference-outcomes}, all published PF tasks pass every measure except for one \emph{Unclear} validity outcome on the hummingbird migration task. Because that task gives each bird's wing beats per minute only as an interval (such as 70 to 80), the resulting daily distances overlap and the winner depends on which values a student picks, so returning \emph{Unclear} rather than \emph{No} matches the task's actual mathematical structure.

    \item \textbf{Rejecting Flawed Tasks for the Right Reason}\\[0.2em]
    Across the lower rows of Figure~\ref{fig:val-reference-outcomes}, none of the flawed tasks passes all five measures, and each group fails on the specific measure its flaw targets. For example, when the published consistency task is modified only by spelling out the variance rule step by step, the task judge quotes \emph{``use exactly this rule''} to return \emph{No} on room for invention and the solver applies that rule directly to return \emph{No} on the limits of familiar approaches, while the one-shot garden task with a geometric contradiction (Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-math-error-example}) is rejected on both familiar limits and mathematical validity.
\end{enumerate}

Across all 29 reference tasks, \textsc{PF Eval} matches every expected outcome, accepting the published PF tasks while rejecting the routine, one-shot, and control tasks on the exact measures they violate. Having verified that the solver finds expected familiar approaches (Section~\ref{subsec:val-solver}) and that the three roles separate working PF tasks from flawed ones, we use \textsc{PF Eval} in Chapter~\ref{chap:results} to compare \textsc{StructPF} against one-shot generation.
