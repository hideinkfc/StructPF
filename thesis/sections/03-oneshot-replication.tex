\section{Reproducing One-Shot PF Generation}
\label{sec:oneshot-replication}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3_1_oneshot_setup.pdf}
    \caption[One-Shot Replication: (a) Single Setting in Rahimi et al.\ versus (b) Four Settings]{One-Shot Replication: (a)~Single Setting in Rahimi et al.~\cite{rahimi2025} versus (b)~Four Settings}
    \label{fig:oneshot-setup}
\end{figure}

The high rubric scores reported by Rahimi et al.~\cite{rahimi2025} ($13.38$ out of $15$ for GPT-4o in Table~\ref{tab:bg-rahimi-results}) suggest at first glance that a single prompt is already sufficient to generate effective PF problems. However, as established in Section~\ref{sec:intro-motivation}, a working PF problem requires more than an engaging story and open-sounding phrasing, as its numbers must be chosen so that familiar methods fail and its wording must leave the core method open for students to invent. To address our first research goal (G1), we therefore examine whether one-shot generated tasks actually satisfy these requirements, or whether the five-dimension rubric rewards surface features while overlooking structural flaws.

\subsection{Multi-Theme Generation Setup}
\label{subsec:oneshot-gen-setup}
Answering this question requires inspecting the generated tasks and their rubric ratings directly. Rahimi et al.~\cite{rahimi2025} published the six-part structure of their prompt, the verbatim five-dimension rubric, and the Grade~7 basketball setting inputs, but did not release the exact prompt wording, the five in-prompt examples, or the generated tasks. We therefore reconstruct the six-part prompt from their published specification and rubric using five human-written PF tasks from the \textsc{StratL} collection~\cite{puech2025} (Table~\ref{tab:app-oneshot-prompt}), and extend the experiment from their single Grade~7 basketball ratio setting to three additional Grade~7 settings covering percents, random-sample distributions, and composite area (Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-setup}b).

While Rahimi et al.~\cite{rahimi2025} compared GPT-4o against an early 3.8B small language model (Phi-3 mini), compact models have since gained built-in chain-of-thought reasoning. We therefore compare a compact reasoning model (GPT-5 mini) against a standard flagship chat model (GPT-4.1) to test whether a smaller model with internal reasoning can match or exceed a larger non-reasoning model under the same one-shot prompt. In each of the four Grade~7 settings, we generate 30 tasks with GPT-5 mini and 30 tasks with GPT-4.1 under default sampling without retries, yielding 240 generation runs in total.

\subsection{Automated Rubric Judge Setup}
\label{subsec:oneshot-judge-setup}
To evaluate all 240 generated tasks consistently, we use an LLM judge (GPT 5.6 Luna) built from the five dimensions and 1--3 scoring anchors of Rahimi et al.~\cite{rahimi2025} (full prompt in Appendix~\ref{app:oneshot-judge}, Table~\ref{tab:app-oneshot-judge}), following their use of an automated LLM rubric judge in \emph{ProductiveMath}~\cite{rahimi2025productivemath}. We keep their rubric wording unchanged so that our results reflect how the original rubric behaves without extra rules or score tuning. Each task is scored in two independent passes without revealing which model generated it, and the two 5--15 totals are averaged to obtain the final score.

\subsection{Rubric Evaluation Results}
\label{subsec:oneshot-results}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig3_2_rubric_comparison.pdf}
    \caption[One-Shot Rubric Scores for GPT-5 mini and GPT-4.1 by (a) Dimension and (b) Setting]{One-Shot Rubric Scores for GPT-5 mini and GPT-4.1 by (a)~Dimension and (b)~Setting}
    \label{fig:oneshot-rubric-results}
\end{figure}

Out of the 240 generation runs, 238 returned valid tasks ($118$ from GPT-5 mini and $120$ from GPT-4.1), with $117$ and $120$ tasks fully scored after one judge call timed out. Across all four settings combined, GPT-5 mini achieves a mean total score of $14.56 \pm 0.49$ out of $15$, outperforming GPT-4.1 at $12.82 \pm 1.23$ with a large effect size ($d = 1.83$). Both models therefore score in the same high range as the $13.38$ total reported for GPT-4o in Table~\ref{tab:bg-rahimi-results}, with GPT-5 mini approaching the 15-point ceiling.

As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-rubric-results}a, both models hit the $3.0$ ceiling on affective engagement and activation of prior knowledge, showing that any task written around the assigned theme and prerequisites satisfies these two criteria. Consistent with the human ratings in Rahimi et al.~\cite{rahimi2025}, the gap between the two models comes almost entirely from open-endedness ($2.69$ vs.\ $1.82$), multiple representations ($2.93$ vs.\ $2.50$), and sweet spot calibration ($2.95$ vs.\ $2.54$). Across the four Grade~7 settings in Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-rubric-results}b, GPT-5 mini stays near the 15-point ceiling on every setting, whereas GPT-4.1 scores above $13.0$ on three settings but drops to $11.35$ on Home and Garden because it ignores the PF design principles in the prompt and gives away the solution setup directly in the task text. At first glance, these high rubric scores suggest that one-shot prompting with a current reasoning model solves PF task generation across settings, which motivates inspecting the generated tasks directly in the next section.

\section{Failure Modes of One-Shot PF Tasks}
\label{sec:oneshot-biases}

To see whether these high-scoring problems actually work as Productive Failure tasks, we inspect all 238 generated tasks against the PF design requirements from Section~\ref{sec:intro-motivation}. Despite the high rubric scores in Section~\ref{subsec:oneshot-results}, three failure modes recur across all four settings. The following three subsections introduce each failure mode with a concrete task example, measure how often it appears across the 238 tasks, and explain why it motivates the dedicated checks in Chapter~\ref{chap:eval-framework}.

\subsection{Tasks Solvable by Familiar Methods}
\label{subsec:routine-story-bias}
The first failure mode is that many one-shot tasks lack a setup where familiar methods fall short, so a routine procedure from an earlier grade solves the problem right away. In the Home and Garden setting, for instance, students have already learned in Grade~6 to find the area of rectangles and right triangles. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-impasse-example}, the generated task presents the garden directly as three separate pieces with all bases and side lengths given, so students simply add the three familiar formulas without facing an unpartitioned figure or a missing dimension.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_3_impasse_example.pdf}
    \caption[One-Shot Task Solvable by Familiar Methods in the Home and Garden Setting]{One-Shot Task Solvable by Familiar Methods in the Home and Garden Setting}
    \label{fig:oneshot-impasse-example}
\end{figure}

Across all 60 Home and Garden tasks, every single task from both models already presents the garden as named rectangles and triangles rather than an unpartitioned figure, yet the rubric still rates sweet spot calibration high because it only asks whether a task looks challenging and manageable on the page rather than whether familiar methods fail. Moreover, a text search can only spot this issue when a geometry prompt names the simple shapes outright. In numerical topics such as Basketball or Shopping, looking at the words alone cannot tell us whether a familiar calculation already solves the problem. Testing that requires working through the given numbers using only the students' prior knowledge, which motivates the prior-knowledge solver we introduce in Chapter~\ref{chap:eval-framework}.

\subsection{Step-by-Step Worksheets and Prescribed Representations}
\label{subsec:helpful-tutor-bias}
\label{subsec:worksheet-bias}
The second failure mode is that the prompt guides students through the solution instead of leaving the approach open for them to invent, primarily in GPT-5 mini. As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-giveaway-example}, the generated basketball task announces the untaught target concept in the opening paragraph and splits the question into a three-part worksheet that tells students what to check in Part~1 and lists five specific representations for them to draw in Part~2.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3_4_giveaway_example.pdf}
    \caption[One-Shot Concept Giveaway and Multi-Part Worksheet in the Basketball Setting]{One-Shot Concept Giveaway and Multi-Part Worksheet in the Basketball Setting}
    \label{fig:oneshot-giveaway-example}
\end{figure}

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:oneshot-hint-worksheet-counts}, GPT-5 mini regularly coaches students through both hints and worksheet structure. Over $80\%$ of its questions name the target concept or suggest specific representations, and $84.7\%$ split the question into three or more numbered parts, more than doubling the median task length to $422.5$ words. Because the generation prompt includes the evaluation rubric (Table~\ref{tab:app-oneshot-prompt}), GPT-5 mini satisfies the top anchors for multiple representations and open-endedness literally by naming representations and stacking multi-part questions. Instead of penalizing this over-guidance, the rubric rewards it with higher scores, which motivates a dedicated check in Chapter~\ref{chap:eval-framework} for whether a task leaves the solution method open to the student.

\subsection{Off-Target Prompts and Mathematical Contradictions}
\label{subsec:offtarget-math-bias}
The third failure mode is that generated tasks often miss part of the target concept or contain contradictions in their numbers and geometry. In the garden task shown in Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-math-error-example}, students are asked to find the area and outer border of a garden made of a central $12\text{ ft} \times 10\text{ ft}$ rectangle with a triangle and a trapezoid attached to its sides. The opening story says that the triangle and the trapezoid sit on opposite long sides of the rectangle, but the measurement list below it attaches the triangle to a $10\text{-ft}$ short side and the trapezoid to a $12\text{-ft}$ long side (Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-math-error-example}a). Because a $10\text{-ft}$ side and a $12\text{-ft}$ side meet at a corner rather than facing each other, the two shapes cannot be on opposite sides, so students cannot draw the garden or find its border consistently (Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-math-error-example}b).

    \caption[One-Shot Mathematical Contradiction in the Home and Garden Setting]{One-Shot Mathematical Contradiction in the Home and Garden Setting}
    \label{fig:oneshot-math-error-example}
\end{figure}

Table~\ref{tab:oneshot-offtarget-math-counts} reports the counts for both task soundness issues. Missing the target concept is clearest in the Music setting, where the Grade~7 goal is to draw conclusions about two large catalogs from small random samples, whereas $90.0\%$ of GPT-4.1 tasks never mention sampling and only ask students to compare two short lists directly, turning the problem back into a Grade~6 exercise. Across all four settings, the rubric judge itself logs at least one entry in its separate mathematical-concerns list (Table~\ref{tab:app-oneshot-judge}) on over $90\%$ of tasks from both models without lowering the rubric score. Because a single concern list mixes genuine contradictions with minor wording notes and never lowers the rubric score, Chapter~\ref{chap:eval-framework} introduces dedicated task soundness checks for target alignment and mathematical validity.

\section{Limitations of Surface Rubric Scoring}
\label{sec:oneshot-surface-rubric}

The gap between the high rubric scores in Section~\ref{sec:oneshot-replication} and the flaws in Section~\ref{sec:oneshot-biases} comes from how the five 1--3 scoring anchors are written (Table~\ref{tab:app-oneshot-judge}). Because the anchors describe general impressions of the text rather than concrete mathematical and structural checks, a generated task can satisfy all five dimensions on the surface while failing the three PF requirements from Section~\ref{sec:intro-motivation} for two main reasons.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=0.6cm,label=\textbf{\arabic*.},itemsep=0.6em,topsep=0.4em]
    \item \textbf{Wording Anchors Reward Hints and Worksheets}\\[0.2em]
    Affective engagement and activation of prior knowledge only require a realistic story and two prerequisite skills, so both reach the ceiling even when a task omits part of the target standard or gives away the formula. For multiple representations and open-endedness, the rubric actively rewards the wrong behavior, in line with known length and formatting biases in LLM judges~\cite{zheng2023judging,wang2024fairevaluators}. Because the top anchors ask for a wide range of representations and multiple variables, a model can raise its score simply by naming which representations students should use and splitting the prompt into a multi-part worksheet. The very instructions that remove student invention are therefore rewarded with higher rubric scores.

    \item \textbf{Text-Only Scoring Never Tests the Math or Familiar Methods}\\[0.2em]
    A second limitation is that the rubric judge only reads the problem text and never works through the numbers or geometry. The sweet spot calibration anchor only asks whether a task looks challenging but manageable on the page, without testing whether familiar methods actually fail. As a result, tasks that already name the component rectangles and triangles directly or contain impossible geometry (Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-math-error-example}) still receive high rubric scores even when the judge records an issue in its separate mathematical-concerns list.
\end{enumerate}

Together, these findings answer our first research goal (G1). Even with modern chat and reasoning models, one-shot generation and direct rubric scoring still treat Productive Failure as a writing style rather than a mathematical design problem. When a single prompt asks a model to write a story, pick the numbers, and follow the rubric at once, even a stronger reasoning model simply optimizes for surface features that are easy to write and easy for a text-only judge to reward. Addressing both issues requires breaking task design and evaluation into separate, checkable steps. In Chapter~\ref{chap:structpf}, we introduce \textsc{StructPF} (G2) to address the generation side by separating prior-knowledge grounding, choosing and checking numbers where familiar methods fail, and writing the final task without hints, before introducing \textsc{PF Eval} (G3), our requirement-based evaluation, in Chapter~\ref{chap:eval-framework}.
