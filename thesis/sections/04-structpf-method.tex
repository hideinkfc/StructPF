\chapter{The \textsc{StructPF} Generation Pipeline}
\label{chap:structpf}

\section{From Human PF Design to Pipeline Architecture}
\label{sec:structpf-overview}

To create a problem that triggers Productive Failure, a human designer does not write a story and choose its numbers in a single step. Instead, as documented by Kapur and Bielaczyc in their design of the Grade~7 average-speed task~\cite{kapur2012}, designing a working PF problem follows a structured sequence of four decisions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=0.7cm,itemsep=0.25em,topsep=0.3em]
    \item \textbf{Identifying prior knowledge:} The designers first inspect earlier curriculum grades to list the concepts and methods students have already been taught, namely speed, ratios and proportions, lowest common multiples, and trial and error from Grades~1 to~6, so that students have familiar tools to start the problem (Requirement~1 in Section~\ref{sec:intro-motivation}).
    \item \textbf{Designing the problem structure:} Next, they choose a problem situation around the critical features of the target concept and anticipate the specific methods students will try using their prior knowledge.
    \item \textbf{Testing and adjusting the numbers:} They then test candidate numbers in pilot sessions and adjust the values so that the target concept holds while familiar shortcut methods, such as taking a simple ratio of the given numbers, fail to give the right answer (Requirement~2).
    \item \textbf{Writing an open problem statement:} Finally, they write the student-facing story and question so that the goal and given quantities are clear, while the solution method is left open for students to invent and explain (Requirement~3).
\end{enumerate}

As we saw in Chapter~\ref{chap:oneshot}, asking an LLM to make all four decisions in a single prompt fails because the model already knows the target formula. It either chooses convenient numbers that familiar shortcuts solve immediately or coaches the student step by step toward the canonical procedure. We have therefore chosen to structure \textsc{StructPF} around the same sequence of steps that a human designer follows, because each decision targets a specific PF mechanism from Section~\ref{subsec:bg-pf-mechanisms} and requires a different kind of check. In order to prevent the model from leaking the target formula or optimizing for the grading criteria, we also keep all planning records and canonical solutions in internal design notes and withhold both the key idea and the evaluation rubric from the final task writer.

    \caption[Three-stage process model of the \textsc{StructPF} generation pipeline]{Three-stage process model of the \textsc{StructPF} pipeline: prerequisite retrieval (Stage~1), design and calculator check (Stage~2), and open task writing (Stage~3).}

An illustration of the entire process is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:structpf-overview}. Every target learning concept undergoes this process, which consists of three stages that mirror the human design workflow. First, Stage~1 queries the Learning Commons Knowledge Graph to identify the prerequisite methods students have already been taught before the target concept (Section~\ref{sec:structpf-d1}). Next, Stage~2 proposes and selects a conceptual design brief in Step~2a and uses a Python math calculator in Step~2b to verify that the chosen numbers make the intended solution work while familiar shortcuts fail (Section~\ref{sec:structpf-d2}). Finally, Stage~3 turns the verified problem setup and numbers into an open student-facing PF task without revealing the solution method (Section~\ref{sec:structpf-d3}). Each step uses a fixed revision limit rather than retrying indefinitely, and Sections~\ref{sec:structpf-d1}--\ref{sec:structpf-d3} trace the Grade~6 polygon-area standard (\texttt{6.G.A.1}) through all three stages.

\section{Stage 1: Retrieving Prerequisite Knowledge}
\label{sec:structpf-d1}

\subsection{Need for External Curriculum Grounding}
\label{subsec:d1-rationale}

Stage~1 must identify the valid methods from earlier grades that students can apply before the target concept is taught (Requirement~1 in Section~\ref{sec:intro-motivation}). When asked directly for prerequisites, however, an LLM already knows the target concept and does not reliably separate earlier-grade skills from it. On a Grade~8 linear-function topic, for example, prompting without curriculum data treated the two-point slope formula $(y_2 - y_1)/(x_2 - x_1)$ as already known rather than keeping the earlier proportional ratio check $y / x$, giving away the core calculation before the task was even written (as we evaluate across 30 topics in Section~\ref{sec:res-grounding}). Stage~1 therefore requires an external curriculum source that explicitly records which skills precede each target standard.

\subsection{The Learning Commons Knowledge Graph}
\label{subsec:d1-learning-commons}

For this curriculum source, we use the open Learning Commons Knowledge Graph (\texttt{v1.14.0}, released under CC~BY~4.0)~\cite{learningcommons2025}, which represents US K--12 Common Core mathematics standards and fine-grained skills as nodes connected by typed relationships. As shown on the left of Figure~\ref{fig:d1-learning-commons}, the graph combines two complementary links around each standard: directional \texttt{buildsTowards} edges from Student Achievement Partners' Coherence Map connect earlier-grade prerequisite standards directly to the later-grade standard they prepare students to learn, while fine-grained \texttt{supports} edges from Achievement Network attach concrete learning components that break broad curriculum statements down into specific student operations.

We have chosen the Learning Commons Knowledge Graph for Stage~1 because its \texttt{buildsTowards} links draw a verifiable line between earlier-grade prerequisites and the target concept, while its \texttt{supports} learning components describe concrete mathematical actions that students can take at the start of a problem.

\subsection{Mapping Graph Nodes to Prior-Skill Records}
\label{subsec:d1-extraction}

An illustration of the Stage~1 process is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:d1-learning-commons}. Given a target Common Core standard, the pipeline follows incoming \texttt{buildsTowards} edges one hop backward to retrieve its direct prerequisite standards and their attached \texttt{supports} learning components (mean $2.6$ parent standards per target across our 30 benchmark topics). We stop at one hop because deeper traversals reach basic elementary arithmetic that is too general to design a focused PF problem. The model then summarizes these retrieved nodes into prior-skill records while keeping the target standard itself off limits: each record pairs an earlier-grade skill with the initial move a student can try and the reason that move falls short, and any candidate that names the target formula is filtered out.

When a topic is given only as free text or comes from a curriculum outside US Common Core (such as our Singapore Grade~7 average-speed, German Grade~10 variance, or undergraduate fair-division tasks), Stage~1 queries the Learning Commons search API with the topic phrase to select the closest proxy standards (for example, mapping Singapore Grade~7 average speed to Common Core unit-rate standards \texttt{6.RP.A.2} and \texttt{7.RP.A.1}). If the graph has no close progression link for the level, as in undergraduate fair division, the pipeline instead retrieves grade-filtered excerpts from local curriculum documents or accepts teacher-supplied prerequisite skills.

\section{Stage 2: Problem Design and Number Verification}
\label{sec:structpf-d2}

\subsection{Proposing and Selecting a Design Brief}
\label{subsec:d2a-brief}

Once Stage~1 has identified what students already know, Stage~2 must design a problem setup where familiar shortcuts fall short and verify its concrete numbers before the problem text is written (Requirement~2 in Section~\ref{sec:intro-motivation}). In Chapter~\ref{chap:oneshot}, asking an LLM to write the prompt directly caused all $30 / 30$ Home and Garden tasks to present already-split shapes that routine formulas solved immediately (Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-impasse-example}). We therefore separate conceptual problem design (Step~2a) from number verification (Step~2b) and task writing (Stage~3), as illustrated for Grade~6 polygon area (\texttt{6.G.A.1}) in Figure~\ref{fig:d2-design-and-check}.

\begin{figure}[!htb]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4_3_stage2_design_and_check.pdf}
    \caption[Stage 2 design brief selection and Python calculator verification]{Stage~2 design brief selection~(a--b) and Python calculator number verification~(c) for Grade~6 polygon area (\texttt{6.G.A.1}).}
    \label{fig:d2-design-and-check}
\end{figure}

Given the Stage~1 prior-skill records, Step~2a generates three independent candidate design briefs (prompts in Tables~\ref{tab:app-structpf-2a-propose}--\ref{tab:app-structpf-2a-translate}) so the pipeline can compare alternative problem structures before choosing numbers. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:d2-design-and-check}a--b, each candidate brief separates the student-facing setup and open question from the withheld internal design notes that record the target key idea, the naive shortcut, and the numerical requirements. A selector model then chooses the candidate that is accessible with Stage~1 skills, causes the simplest Stage~1 shortcut to fall short, and avoids method giveaways in the student-facing fields, returning at most two revision calls if a concern remains before passing the brief to Step~2b.

\subsection{Selecting and Verifying Task Numbers}
\label{subsec:d2b-numbers}

To turn the selected design brief into a working problem, we need to choose concrete numbers for the quantities in the task. The goal is to ensure that the familiar shortcut fails while the geometry and the intended solution remain valid. We have chosen to use a Python calculator for this step because an LLM often picks numbers where a shortcut already works or introduces impossible dimensions when writing a problem directly (Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-math-error-example}). For this sub-process we use \texttt{SymPy}~\cite{meurer2017sympy} to evaluate arithmetic, square roots, list totals, and equalities or inequalities. In order to keep the final answer out of the student task, we also label each number as either a student-visible given value or a withheld internal value.

An illustration of this verification process is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:d2-design-and-check}c. Every selected design brief undergoes this process (prompts summarized in Tables~\ref{tab:app-structpf-2b-propose}--\ref{tab:app-structpf-2b-repair}), which has two parts. In the first part, the model proposes concrete numbers for the quantities in the brief and separates the brief's requirements into computable conditions and qualitative rules. Only the computable conditions are translated into mathematical expressions for the calculator, whereas qualitative rules, such as keeping a figure unsplit, are checked directly against the brief. The second part of the process entails verifying the computable conditions in the Python calculator. If any condition is not satisfied, the calculator returns the computed values and the failed check to the model so that the numbers can be adjusted, repeating this step for at most two repair rounds. Finally, once all conditions hold, the verified student-visible values are passed to Stage~3, as illustrated for the play-area floor task in Table~\ref{tab:d2b-number-regimes}.

\section{Stage 3: Open-Ended Task Generation}
\label{sec:structpf-d3}

To complete the task design, we need to turn the verified problem setup and numbers from Stage~2 into a clear student-facing problem while leaving the solution method open for students to invent (Requirement~3 in Section~\ref{sec:intro-motivation}). The Stage~3 writer prompt is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:app-structpf-3-write}. As we saw in Chapter~\ref{chap:oneshot}, when an LLM sees the target concept and the five-dimension evaluation rubric while writing the task, it frequently coaches the student by naming specific representations (Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-giveaway-example}) or breaking the problem into a step-by-step worksheet (Figure~\ref{fig:oneshot-worksheet-example}). We have therefore chosen to place an information barrier between Stage~2 and Stage~3, because a language model that sees the canonical solution or the grading rubric when writing the task turns internal planning notes into student hints.

\end{figure}

An illustration of Stage~3 and the resulting task for our Grade~6 polygon-area example (\texttt{6.G.A.1}) is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:d3-task-writer}. Passing only the student-facing setup and the verified student-visible numbers across the barrier (Figure~\ref{fig:d3-task-writer}a) produces a concise task (\emph{``Two Play-Area Floor Designs''}, Figure~\ref{fig:d3-task-writer}b) that defines the comparison clearly while keeping the polygons unsplit and leaving the solution strategy to the student.

By separating prerequisite retrieval (Stage~1), problem design and calculator verification (Stage~2), and open-ended task writing (Stage~3), \textsc{StructPF} mirrors the four decisions of a human PF designer while keeping internal planning notes and evaluation rubrics out of the student-facing prompt. To test whether the generated tasks actually resist familiar shortcuts and elicit multiple student approaches, Chapter~\ref{chap:eval-framework} introduces \textsc{PF Eval}.
